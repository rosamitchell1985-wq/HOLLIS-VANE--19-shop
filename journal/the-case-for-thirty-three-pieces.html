Wardrobe

The case for thirty-three pieces

We asked eleven customers to count everything they own and wear. What came back said more about storage and laundry than about style.

By Teodor Vane 4 February 2026 1,210 words

In this piece

  • What we asked, and why
  • The counts
  • The laundry constraint
  • What people actually wore
  • The thirty-three number
  • What this does not prove

Written in the studio at 214 Lafayette Street. No sponsorship, no affiliate links, no gifted product.

In November 2025 we asked eleven customers to do something tedious. Count every garment you own. Then count every garment you have actually worn in the last ninety days. Send both numbers, plus a short note about what surprised you.

All eleven had bought from us at least twice. Six live in New York, two in Chicago, one each in Portland, Austin and Providence. Ages ranged from 29 to 61. This is not a representative sample of anything and we are not going to pretend otherwise. It was eleven people who were willing to spend an evening counting socks.

What we asked, and why

The reason for asking is commercial and we may as well say so. We make twenty-eight styles. Every time we consider adding a twenty-ninth, the honest question is whether it replaces something in the customer's wardrobe or simply joins it. If it joins it, we are making the problem worse and taking money for the privilege.

We defined a garment as anything worn on the body excluding underwear, socks, and anything purely athletic. Coats counted. Shoes counted. Scarves counted. A drawer of identical white T-shirts counted as however many T-shirts were in the drawer, which turned out to matter.

The counts

Total garments owned ranged from 74 to 341. The median was 129. Garments worn in the previous ninety days ranged from 21 to 58, with a median of 34.

Put plainly: the median person in this group owned 129 things and had worn 34 of them in three months. That is 26%. The person who owned 341 had worn 44, which is 13%. The person who owned 74 had worn 31, which is 42% and the highest ratio in the group.

Two people volunteered that they had counted twice because they did not believe the first number. One of them found nineteen garments still in a suitcase from a move in 2023.

The pattern held across every participant regardless of total wardrobe size: the number of garments in active rotation clustered between 30 and 40. Owning more did not raise that number. It only raised the number sitting still.

The laundry constraint

The most useful thing that came out of the exercise was not about taste at all. It was about laundry.

Four participants independently described the same mechanism. The number of shirts or T-shirts they keep is set by how often they do laundry, not by how many they like. One participant put it exactly: she washes on Sundays, wears roughly five shirts a week, wants a buffer of two, and therefore keeps seven. She owns twenty-three. The other sixteen exist because she bought replacements before disposing of predecessors.

This is a genuinely useful frame. Your active wardrobe size is a function of your laundry cycle multiplied by your wear rate, plus a small buffer. Everything above that number is storage, not wardrobe. It is not a moral failing, it is just inventory, and it has a cost in space, in decision time and in the ten minutes every morning spent moving past things you are not going to wear.

Two participants noted the corollary, which is that buying better-made clothing only helps if you also reduce the count. A durable shirt worn once a fortnight lasts a very long time and gets no chance to prove it.

What people actually wore

We asked for the ninety-day list in categories. Across eleven people the medians looked like this.

  • Tops, including shirts, knitwear and tees: 12
  • Bottoms, including trousers, denim and skirts: 5
  • Outer layers, including coats, jackets and blazers: 4
  • Dresses and one-piece garments: 2
  • Shoes: 4
  • Accessories, including scarves, belts and bags: 5

That totals 32. Add the two or three genuinely occasional items every participant listed separately, a formal coat, a suit, one pair of proper shoes, and the working number lands between 33 and 35.

The concentration in outer layers was the finding that surprised us most. Four coats covered every condition from a New York January to a Portland spring, and in nine of eleven cases one coat accounted for more than half of all outer-layer wears. People are effectively monogamous about coats and then own six of them.

The thirty-three number

Thirty-three is not a rule and we are not proposing a challenge, a capsule programme or a cleanse. It is simply where this small group landed when they counted honestly, and it is close enough to other published wardrobe audits that it is probably not a coincidence.

What is useful about it is what it implies for buying. If the working wardrobe is around thirty-three pieces and the median person owns 129, then almost every purchase any of us makes is an addition rather than a replacement. The garment does not enter the rotation. It enters storage next to the other ninety-six.

The only reliable way we know to break that is the test we use when designing: does this replace something, and if so, what. Not conceptually. Specifically. Which garment currently in your rotation stops being worn when this one arrives? If the answer is none, the new thing is not joining your wardrobe, it is joining your inventory.

This is inconvenient advice for a company that sells clothing, and we are aware of that. It also happens to be the only argument we have for why anyone should pay $420 for a sweater.

What this does not prove

Eleven self-selected people who already buy from a small monochrome label are not a study. They are unusually likely to think about this in the first place, which is exactly the bias you would expect. A group of eleven people who buy fast fashion weekly would produce different numbers, and the interesting version of this exercise is the one we cannot run.

The ninety-day window also flatters the count, because it excludes seasonal clothing entirely. Ask in July about a wool overcoat and it will not appear on a ninety-day list despite being worn heavily for five months of the year. Our participants noted this themselves and several sent revised numbers accounting for it, which is what pushed the working figure from around 30 up towards 35.

What we take from it is narrow and we will not stretch it further: people wear about a third of what they own, the active number is remarkably stable, and adding to it mostly does not change it. That is enough to keep the collection at twenty-eight styles.
