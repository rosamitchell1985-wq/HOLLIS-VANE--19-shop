How to read a wool label

Merino, lambswool, virgin wool, recycled wool. Four words that appear on almost every knitwear label in America and mean four different things for how a garment will age.

By Marguerite Hollis 12 March 2026 1,180 words

In this piece

  • The four words on every label
  • Micron, and why it is not warmth
  • Staple length, the number nobody prints
  • Ply and gauge
  • Reading the care symbols
  • A short shopping test

Written in the studio at 214 Lafayette Street. No sponsorship, no affiliate links, no gifted product.

A knitwear label in an American store is legally required to tell you the fibre content by percentage and the country of manufacture. Everything else on it is optional, which means the useful information is usually missing and the information that is present is easy to misread. Here is what the words actually mean, in the order you will meet them.

The four words on every label

Virgin wool means the fibre has never been used before. It is not a quality claim. A coarse, scratchy fleece is still virgin wool. What it rules out is reprocessed fibre, which is worth knowing but tells you nothing about how the sweater will feel in month six.

Lambswool is the first shearing from a sheep, usually taken at around seven months. The fibres are finer and softer than the same animal's later fleeces, and they have not yet been weathered. It is genuinely better, and it is also finite: a sheep produces one lamb's fleece in its life.

Merino refers to a breed, not a grade. Merino sheep grow unusually fine fleece, which is why the word has become shorthand for soft. But merino ranges from around 24 microns, which will feel scratchy against the neck, down to 15 microns, which will not. A label that says merino and nothing else has told you the breed and withheld the number that matters.

Recycled wool is fibre recovered from post-industrial offcuts or post-consumer garments, shredded and respun. The shredding shortens the staple, so recycled wool is almost always blended with virgin fibre to give the yarn enough strength to hold together. It has a real environmental case and a real durability cost, and any brand telling you it has neither is selling you something.

Micron, and why it is not warmth

Micron count is the average diameter of the fibre. Under about 17.5 microns, most people cannot feel individual fibre ends against the skin at the neck and wrist, which is what we experience as itch. Above about 22 microns, most people can. The threshold varies by person, which is why one friend swears by a sweater that makes you miserable.

Micron does not measure warmth. Warmth comes from trapped air, which is a function of how much yarn is in the garment and how it is knitted, not how fine the fibre is. A 3 gauge lambswool fisherman knit at 24 microns will be far warmer than a 12 gauge merino at 16 microns, and far scratchier. Both facts are true at once and neither one makes the other garment worse.

Our own numbers, for reference: the cashmere in The Cashmere Crew is 15.5 microns. The merino in the turtleneck and the polo knit is 19.5. The undyed lambswool in the fisherman sweater is around 26, and it is meant to be worn over something.

Staple length, the number nobody prints

Staple length is how long each individual fibre is before it is spun. It is the single best predictor of pilling and almost never appears on a label. Long fibres have fewer ends per inch of yarn, so fewer ends can work their way to the surface, tangle with their neighbours and form a pill.

For cashmere, 34 mm and above is considered long. Below 30 mm you are buying something that will felt into small hard balls by the second winter, regardless of what the percentage on the label says. This is the main reason a $99 cashmere sweater and a $420 one can both say one hundred percent cashmere and behave completely differently. The percentage is honest. It is just not the relevant number.

If a brand does not publish staple length, you can ask. The answer, or the absence of one, is informative. We publish ours because we had to argue with our spinner to get it, and having argued we may as well use it.

Ply and gauge

Ply is how many strands are twisted together to make the yarn. Two-ply is twisted, balanced and resistant to abrasion. Single-ply is one strand, which is lighter and cheaper and considerably more fragile. A two-ply cashmere at 7 gauge is roughly twice the yarn of a single-ply at the same gauge, which is why it costs roughly twice as much and lasts several times longer.

Gauge is the number of needles per inch on the knitting machine. A 12 gauge is fine and smooth and works under a jacket. A 7 gauge has visible loops and body. A 3 gauge is sculptural and heavy. None is better than another; they solve different rooms and different temperatures.

Between them, ply and gauge tell you more about the physical object than the fibre name does. Two-ply, 7 gauge, 36 mm staple is a sweater you will still own in ten years. Single-ply, 12 gauge, unnamed staple is a sweater for this year.

Reading the care symbols

The symbols are standardised and almost nobody reads them, so here are the four that matter for wool.

  • A tub with a hand in it means hand wash only, cool water, no agitation.
  • A tub with a number and one bar underneath means a gentle machine cycle at that temperature. Two bars means very gentle, which most machines call wool or delicate.
  • A circle with a P or an F means dry clean only, with the letter telling the cleaner which solvent to use.
  • A square with a horizontal line inside means dry flat. On knitwear this is not a suggestion. A wet sweater on a hanger will stretch under its own weight and never fully recover.

Most knitwear labelled dry clean only can in fact be washed on a wool cycle at 30 degrees. Brands print dry clean because it transfers the risk to you and the cleaner. We print the actual instruction, because the failure mode of dry cleaning cashmere every month is that the solvent strips the natural oils and the fibre goes brittle within a couple of years.

A short shopping test

Standing in a shop with a sweater in your hands, three checks take about fifteen seconds.

  1. Hold it up to a window. If you can see through the knit clearly, it is a fine gauge or a thin yarn, or both. That is fine for layering and poor for warmth.
  2. Stretch the cuff and let go. It should return. A cuff that stays wide has too little rib or too little twist in the yarn, and it will be baggy within a season.
  3. Look at the shoulder seam from the inside. Fully fashioned knitwear is knitted to shape and linked stitch by stitch, so the seam is flat and follows a visible line of loops. Cut-and-sew knitwear has an overlocked seam like a T-shirt, which means panels were cut from a knitted sheet. The second method is faster, cheaper and the reason a sweater unravels at the shoulder in year three.

None of this requires a technical background and none of it depends on price. Plenty of expensive knitwear fails all three tests. What it does require is that the brand publish the numbers, which most do not, and which is the actual argument of this article.
