Supply chain

Where our cashmere comes from

From a herding cooperative in Alashan to a knitting frame in Dumfries, including the two parts of the journey we cannot fully verify.

By Marguerite Hollis 16 January 2026 1,240 words

In this piece

  • Combing, not shearing
  • The auction, and what it hides
  • Dehairing
  • Spinning in Todmorden
  • Knitting in Dumfries
  • What we cannot verify
  • What we do about it

Written in the studio at 214 Lafayette Street. No sponsorship, no affiliate links, no gifted product.

A cashmere sweater has a longer supply chain than almost anything else we make. Ours passes through at least six sets of hands across four countries before it reaches a box in New Jersey. Here is the route, including the two stages where our knowledge is genuinely incomplete.

Combing, not shearing

Cashmere is the soft undercoat a goat grows for winter and sheds in spring. It is combed out by hand, not sheared, over a period of several weeks as the animal moults. A single goat yields between 150 and 200 grams of raw fibre a year. After the coarse guard hair is removed, roughly half of that remains.

The sweater you can buy from us takes the annual yield of four to five goats. That ratio is the single fact that explains the price of cashmere and also explains why cheap cashmere exists: the way to make it cheaper is to shear rather than comb, which is faster and takes guard hair with it, or to buy shorter fibre from the second and third combings.

Our fibre comes from the Alashan League in western Inner Mongolia, through a herding cooperative that our spinner has bought from since 2009. Alashan fibre is prized because the winters are severe and the undercoat grows fine and long in response. The cooperative covers 140 households.

The auction, and what it hides

Raw cashmere is traditionally sold at regional auction, where fibre from many herds is graded, mixed and sold in lots. This is where traceability normally dies. Once fibre from forty herds is combined into a graded lot, no downstream buyer can tell you which animal, which household or which grazing land it came from.

We buy through a spinner that takes fibre from the cooperative under a direct contract rather than at open auction, which is why we can name the region and the household count. This costs more per kilogram, in our case about 11% more than the equivalent auction grade, and it is the main reason our cashmere costs what it does before anyone has knitted anything.

It is worth being precise about what this buys. It buys us origin at the level of a cooperative. It does not buy us a farm-by-farm audit, and anyone claiming that level of granularity across a herding region of this size should be asked how.

Dehairing

Raw combed cashmere is around 40% guard hair by weight. Removing it is a mechanical process done in China, close to the source, because shipping guard hair around the world is pointless. The fibre goes through a series of rotating combs that separate by diameter and length.

Dehairing is where a great deal of quality is won or lost, and it is almost never discussed. Aggressive dehairing produces a cleaner fibre faster and breaks more of it, shortening the average staple. Gentle dehairing preserves length and costs more time. Our lots come out at an average staple of 36 mm, which we have independently tested twice at a fibre laboratory in Ilkley, because the alternative was taking a number on trust.

Spinning in Todmorden

The dehaired fibre travels by sea to England, where it is scoured, dyed if it is going to be dyed, and spun into a two-ply worsted yarn at a mill in Todmorden, West Yorkshire, that has been spinning cashmere since 1846.

Two things happen here that affect the finished sweater more than most people realise. The first is twist. More twist makes a stronger, harder yarn that pills less and feels less soft; less twist makes a yarn that feels wonderful in the shop and pills within a month. We specify a middling twist, deliberately, and it is a compromise we would defend but not pretend is free.

The second is dyeing. Cashmere is dyed as loose fibre or as yarn, and yarn dyeing gives a more even result. Our black is a yarn dye. Dyeing is also the most water-intensive stage of the whole chain, which is one of the reasons the collection is limited to two colours rather than eight.

Knitting in Dumfries

The spun yarn goes by road to Glenlaurie Knitwear in Dumfries, Scotland, where it is knitted on 7 gauge frames into fully fashioned panels. Fully fashioned means each panel is shaped on the machine by adding and removing stitches, rather than cut out of a knitted sheet. The panels are then linked together stitch by stitch on a hand-operated linking machine, which is a skilled job and takes about forty minutes per sweater.

After linking, the sweater is washed in Dumfries water, which is soft, and that wash is what gives Scottish knitwear its characteristic bloom. It is not marketing. Hard water leaves mineral deposits in the fibre and the difference is immediately obvious side by side.

The finished sweater is inspected, pressed, folded and shipped by sea to our warehouse in Secaucus, New Jersey.

What we cannot verify

Two gaps, stated plainly.

First, grazing practice. Cashmere goats are hard on grassland, and overgrazing in parts of Inner Mongolia and Mongolia has contributed to serious desertification over three decades. Our cooperative participates in a rangeland management programme with stocking limits, and we have seen the documentation. We have not personally verified stocking rates on the ground, and we have never visited Alashan. That is a real limitation and we are not going to describe a document review as a visit.

Second, animal welfare during combing. Combing is not inherently harmful and is done during natural moult, but practice varies and welfare at the household level in a 140-household cooperative is not something a certificate can meaningfully guarantee. The Good Cashmere Standard our fibre is certified against does include welfare criteria and third-party inspection of a sample of farms. A sample is not all of them.

What we do about it

Three concrete things, none of them heroic.

  • We buy under direct contract rather than at auction, and we pay the premium that requires, so that origin is knowable at all.
  • We test staple length independently rather than accepting the mill figure, because that number determines whether the sweater lasts.
  • We repair cashmere free for life, which is the intervention with the largest effect. A reknitted moth hole in a 2019 sweater is one sweater that does not need to be made. We did 61 cashmere repairs in 2025.

What we do not do is describe any of this as sustainable. It is a long supply chain for a luxury fibre produced by animals in a fragile grassland, and the most environmentally sound version of this sweater is the one you already own. Our argument is narrower: if you are going to buy one, buy one built to be repaired, and buy it from someone willing to write down what they do not know.
